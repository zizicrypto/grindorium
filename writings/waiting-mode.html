Waiting Mode

The appointment is at 3 p.m. It is 9 a.m., and the day is already over. You cannot start the project, cannot run the errand, cannot settle into anything, because some part of your brain has designated this a waiting day, and waiting is now the day's only program. Six perfectly good hours will be spent in a low-grade hover, checking the time, doing fragments of nothing, guarding an event that needs no guarding. People who live this call it waiting mode, and if the term sounds informal, that is because it is, a community-coined name, especially common in ADHD circles, for an experience formal research has barely touched. The experience itself is anything but rare.

What seems to be happening, mechanically, is a vigilance hijack. An upcoming commitment becomes an open loop the mind refuses to set down, and for brains that struggle with time perception or working memory, the refusal is rational in its own way: if I stop actively holding this appointment, I will lose it. So a background process becomes a foreground one. The appointment does not take an hour of your day. It takes custody of all of it, because the mind is spending continuous resources making sure 3 p.m. does not escape.

Time blindness deepens the trap. If you cannot feel durations reliably, the six hours before the appointment do not register as six usable hours. They register as before the thing, a single undifferentiated zone, too short for anything real, since starting something means risking absorption, and absorption is exactly how appointments get missed. The fear is not irrational. It is overcorrected. The system that once made you forget things entirely now refuses to let you remember them at any sane cost.

There is also a quieter component: transitions are expensive. Entering a task costs ignition, and leaving one mid-flow to switch into appointment mode costs again. A brain that knows both tolls sometimes refuses the round trip and idles instead, paying with the whole day to avoid paying twice. The math is bad, but it is math, not laziness, and seeing it as math is what makes it solvable.

What helps is taking the custody job away from your mind and giving it to machinery. Set two alarms, one at the leave-now moment and one thirty minutes before it, with full trust placed in them, because waiting mode persists exactly as long as you are the backup system. Then deliberately fill the protected gap with tasks from a pre-made before-appointments list, things that are absorbing enough to use the time but interruptible by design, tidying, admin, errands near the venue, workouts, not the deep work that punishes interruption. Stacking helps too: schedule appointments at the edges of days or back to back, so the waiting zones overlap instead of each claiming its own day.

And drop the shame line item entirely. A day lost to waiting mode was not wasted by weakness. It was consumed by an overactive guarding system doing, with enormous inefficiency, a job you never delegated properly. Delegate it, to alarms, to lists, to structure, and the hours come back. The appointment was only ever going to need fifteen minutes of you. The rest of the day was always yours. Now it can be again.
