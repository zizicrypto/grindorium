Ego Depletion

Ego depletion refers to the reduction in capacity for self-regulation that occurs after sustained effort, decision-making, or resisting impulses. The concept was introduced by psychologist Roy Baumeister and has been widely studied, though the precise mechanism remains debated.

The core observation is practical: after making many decisions, resisting temptations, or exerting sustained self-control, people are more likely to make impulsive choices, give in to impulses they would otherwise resist, and perform poorly on tasks requiring effort or concentration.

This explains why willpower-based approaches to discipline so often fail. Willpower is not a stable trait, it is a resource that depletes. Relying on it as the primary mechanism for behavior change ignores the structural reality of how it actually works.

Practical implications: important decisions are better made earlier in the day when cognitive resources are higher. Environments that reduce the need for willpower (by removing temptations or reducing choices) are more effective than environments that require constant resistance.

The most effective behavioral systems reduce dependence on willpower rather than demanding more of it.
