Masking

Someone asks how you are and the machinery engages before you decide anything. The face arranges itself, the voice finds its pleasant register, the word fine arrives on schedule. Inside, something entirely different is happening, exhaustion, dread, a sadness with no slot in the workday, but none of it reaches the surface, because the surface is managed. This is masking, the continuous performance of okay, and the people best at it have usually been doing it so long they only notice the mask in the moment after it comes off, alone in the car, when the face finally falls.

A note on the word first, because it carries two related meanings. In autistic and neurodivergent communities, masking specifically means camouflaging natural traits to pass in neurotypical settings, a heavy, well-documented burden of its own. This piece is about the broader version that overlaps with it: the everyday concealment of true emotional states behind an acceptable display. Different origins, same core transaction, the inside hidden, the outside curated, the gap maintained at personal cost.

That cost has been studied directly. Sociologist Arlie Hochschild, researching flight attendants, named the work of managing displayed feelings emotional labor and distinguished surface acting, faking the expression while the real feeling stays underneath, from deep acting, where you work to actually change the feeling. Decades of workplace research since have found surface acting the more corrosive of the two, reliably linked with emotional exhaustion and burnout. Holding a smile over a contrary interior is not nothing. It is sustained muscular and cognitive work, performed on top of whatever the day already demands.

The deeper cost arrives slowly and is harder to bill. Feelings that are never expressed get harder to access. Mask long enough and the question how are you stops having a private answer too, not because you are hiding it, because the reporting line inward went quiet from disuse. People at this stage describe feeling like an actor with no offstage, or realizing that no one, anywhere, knows them, which is technically true, since what everyone knows is the performance. The mask was built to protect connection, and its final trick is to make real connection impossible, because connection requires being seen and the mask exists to prevent exactly that.

It is worth saying that masking is not a character flaw and is often rational. Workplaces punish visible struggle. Some families never made room for honest feeling. Some situations genuinely require composure. The problem is not that the mask exists. It is that it stopped being removable, that there is no person and no place where the face is allowed to tell the truth.

What helps is not dramatic unmasking everywhere, which is neither safe nor necessary. It is re-establishing offstage. One person with whom the answer to how are you is accurate. One channel, a journal counts, where the inside gets stated in words without management. Brief daily contact with your own actual state, asked privately, what is true in here right now, even if nothing is done about the answer. The skill being rebuilt is not honesty with the world. It is access to yourself, which masking quietly cut.

The mask can stay in the toolkit. It earned its place. It just cannot be the face, and the difference between the two is whether somewhere, regularly, it comes off while someone, even just you, is watching.
