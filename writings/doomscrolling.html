Doomscrolling

It is late, you are tired, and you are reading the worst news available, on purpose, one swipe at a time. Crisis, outrage, collapse, repeat. You are not enjoying it. You feel worse with every screen, and you keep going, as if somewhere further down the feed there is a final piece of bad news that will complete the picture and let you stop. There is not. The feed has no bottom, and that is the design.

Doomscrolling runs on machinery older than phones. Human attention carries a negativity bias, threats register faster and stick harder than good news, because the ancestors who ignored rustling grass did not become ancestors. A brain built to monitor danger meets an infinite scroll of danger and does exactly what it was built to do: keeps monitoring. Forever, if allowed. The phone did not create the instinct. It built the instinct a treadmill.

The behavior feels like staying informed, which is its best disguise. There is real news in there, and knowing things matters. But notice the shape of the sessions. Information seeking has a stopping point, you learn the thing, you stop. Doomscrolling has no stopping point, because it is not driven by curiosity. It is driven by vigilance, the feeling that looking away is when the bad thing happens. Checking soothes the alarm for a few seconds, the alarm returns, you check again. That loop is not a news habit. It is a threat ritual.

Studies during recent global crises linked heavy doomscrolling with higher anxiety and worse mood, which surprises no one who has done it. The subtler cost is calibration. A feed concentrates the worst events of an entire planet into one thumb-width column, and a nervous system cannot tell the difference between witnessed danger and personal danger. You end the session with a body that believes the world outside the door matches the world inside the screen. It does not, and living as if it does is corrosive.

There is also the numbness on the far side. Enough exposure and the horror flattens into wallpaper. People notice they can swipe past a catastrophe with nothing registering and feel disturbed by their own flatness. That flatness is not callousness. It is a saturation response, a system protecting itself the only way left. But it leaks. The mute button does not apply selectively, and the things you wanted to feel get quieter too.

What helps is replacing the open loop with a closed one. Decide how you will be informed, a specific source, a specific time, once or twice a day, and let that be the agreement. The vigilance will object at first, that is withdrawal talking, not responsibility. Make the scroll harder to start, the app off the home screen, the phone out of the bedroom, friction is not weakness, it is architecture. And when the urge hits at midnight, name what it actually is: not a need to know, a need to feel safe. Then do the thing that addresses safety, breath, warmth, sleep, a real person, because the feed was never going to provide it.

You can know what is happening in the world without holding the world's pain in your hand every hour. Informed has a finish line. Make yourself one.
