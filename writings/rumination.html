Rumination

Rumination is the repetitive, passive focusing on symptoms of distress and their possible causes and consequences. It is distinct from constructive problem-solving: where problem-solving moves toward resolution, rumination circles the same content without progress.

Rumination is one of the strongest predictors of depression and anxiety. Research consistently shows that people who ruminate frequently have longer and more severe depressive episodes, more difficulty recovering from stress, and higher overall levels of psychological distress.

The brain ruminates because it is trying to solve a problem. The loop feels productive, like working through something. But rumination typically does not lead to insight or resolution. It leads to increased negative affect and entrenchment of negative beliefs.

Common triggers include perceived failure or rejection, uncertainty, interpersonal conflict, and loss. The ruminative thinking circles the triggering event searching for understanding or control that is not available.

Interrupting rumination is more effective than trying to resolve the content. Physical activity, behavioral engagement, and mindfulness practices that redirect attention without suppression are among the most evidence-supported approaches.
