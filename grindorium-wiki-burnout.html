Burnout

Burnout is not a breakdown. It is what happens when you keep going long after the signal to stop. Understanding burnout is the first step to something different.

What is burnout?

Burnout is a state of chronic exhaustion — physical, emotional, and mental — that results from sustained overload without adequate recovery. It was formally recognized by the World Health Organization in 2019 as an occupational phenomenon.

But burnout extends beyond work. It can come from caregiving, creative work, relationships, or any sustained effort in an environment that does not give back.

You did not run out of energy. You ran out of reasons.

The three dimensions of burnout

Exhaustion

The depletion of emotional and physical resources. Not tiredness that sleep resolves — a deeper emptiness that persists even after rest.

Cynicism and detachment

A distancing from work, people, or purpose that once mattered. A protective numbness that develops when engagement costs too much.

Reduced efficacy

The sense that effort no longer produces results — that you are less capable than you once were, or that what you do no longer matters.

Signs of burnout

  • Waking up exhausted regardless of how much you slept
  • Difficulty finding meaning or motivation in things that used to matter
  • Emotional flatness — neither strong positive nor negative reactions
  • Irritability disproportionate to circumstances
  • Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
  • Physical symptoms: headaches, stomach issues, lowered immunity
  • Withdrawing from people and activities you used to enjoy

What causes burnout?

Burnout rarely has a single cause. Research identifies six key mismatches that contribute:

  • Workload that consistently exceeds capacity
  • Lack of control over how work gets done
  • Insufficient recognition or reward
  • Breakdown in community or belonging
  • Perceived unfairness in treatment or outcomes
  • Values conflict — doing work that contradicts who you are

Burnout vs. depression

Burnout and depression overlap but are distinct. Burnout is typically context-specific — it improves significantly when the stressor is removed or changed. Depression is more pervasive, affecting all areas of life regardless of context.

Both deserve attention. If symptoms persist after removing the stressor, speaking with a professional matters.
