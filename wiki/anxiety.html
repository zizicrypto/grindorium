Anxiety

Anxiety is a protection system that has been activated too long. Understanding how it works changes how you relate to it.

What is anxiety?

Anxiety is the body's threat response system — a state of physiological and psychological activation designed to prepare for perceived danger. It becomes problematic when it activates persistently in the absence of real threat, or when the response is disproportionate to the actual risk.

Your nervous system is not broken. It is doing exactly what it was designed to do. The problem is the signal, not the system.

Anxiety vs. fear vs. worry

Fear

An immediate response to a specific, present danger. Subsides when the danger passes.

Anxiety

Anticipatory. Focused on potential future threat. Can persist even when no immediate danger exists.

Worry

A cognitive activity — repetitive thinking about potential problems. Worry is a symptom of anxiety, not the same thing.

How anxiety shows up in the body

  • Increased heart rate and shallow breathing
  • Muscle tension, particularly in the jaw, shoulders, and chest
  • Hypervigilance — scanning for threats, difficulty relaxing
  • Digestive disruption — nausea, stomach tension
  • Sleep difficulty — trouble falling or staying asleep
  • Fatigue from sustained activation

Common anxiety patterns

Generalized anxiety

Persistent worry across multiple domains of life. The content shifts but the underlying state of activation remains.

Social anxiety

Fear of negative evaluation in social situations. Often involves anticipatory anxiety before events and reviewing afterward.

Health anxiety

Preoccupation with illness or physical symptoms. Often involves reassurance-seeking and medical checking.

Performance anxiety

Anxiety specifically triggered by situations requiring evaluation or performance — exams, presentations, interviews.

What drives anxiety?

Anxiety often develops from early experiences where threat was unpredictable or where the person lacked control or safety. The nervous system learns to stay alert. Over time, the alertness becomes the default state rather than the response.

Avoidance maintains anxiety. Each time you avoid something that triggers anxiety, the threat signal is reinforced. The short-term relief makes the long-term pattern stronger.
